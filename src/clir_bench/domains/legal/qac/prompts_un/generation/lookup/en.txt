You create UN lookup retrieval questions. The searcher knows the source and needs one precise point from it. A source locator is part of this information need. Do not prefer this mode's facts over equally useful specialist or conceptual questions.
SOURCE AND EVIDENCE
The input has TARGET BLOCK, REFERENCED DOCUMENTS, and DOCUMENT CONTEXT sections, plus source metadata. Questions concern the target block. Supporting text may resolve names, speakers, regimes, dates and references for the question; it cannot supply missing answer substance. Every answer must be a contiguous target-block span that fully resolves its question when read with that question. Include necessary conditions, exceptions, modality and attribution; omit unrelated material. Several adjacent clauses or sentences are acceptable when needed for one coherent answer. Do not stitch passages, invent explanations, or answer with a bare yes/no. Resolving a referent already identified by the question is not missing evidence.
Preserve the source's status: a request is a request, a proposal is a proposal, an authorization is not implementation, and an attributed allegation is not established fact. Attribute claims to the relevant State or office. Do not substitute the document's sender, chair or recipient for the actual speaker. A quoted provision can support a question about this source's application, interpretation or reported position; do not turn it into an unattributed general-law question whose better source is the quoted instrument.
USEFULNESS AND CONTEXT
Use concrete measures, deadlines, conditions, exceptions, mandates, procedures, reported facts, assessments or stated rationales. Non-binding requests and proposals can be useful when their content and status are explicit. Pure ceremony, an unspecified appeal, or a pointer to missing substance is insufficient. Judge the proposition, not isolated verbs such as “recalls”, “welcomes” or “requests”. Recurring wording is not automatically unusable: check whether the question identifies the relevant regime, reporting instance, measure or period. Do not assume unseen duplicates; do not require a norm to change every year to ask what applied then.
The question must supply enough substantive context and relevant absolute time to identify the need among plausible alternatives. One distinctive composite anchor may suffice; extra generic nouns do not improve identification. Resolve essential definite references. Use a year, date or dated period from the supplied material to distinguish the intended instance; do not turn the document date into an unsupported event or effective date. Avoid unresolved “current”, “next session” or “the reporting period”. Where the requested date is unknown, pin the inquiry to the relevant decision, event or reporting period rather than giving away the answer.
WORDING
Write questions and answers in English. Preserve official names, legal terms, figures, dates, units and material qualifications. Paraphrase ordinary scaffolding where natural, without changing the meaning. Necessary legal terminology and shared question/answer terms are not copying defects or answer leakage. A yes/no question is useful when the evidence determines a genuinely unknown status or condition; its deciding span adds information even if it shares the question's words. Do not guess what a specialist already knows. Reject circular questions that actually contain their answer.
Prefer concise natural questions, usually 8–25 words. This is a guideline: retain necessary scope, attribution and qualifications even when longer. Avoid padding, independent bundled requests and artificial paraphrases. Generate up to THREE different questions; fewer is fine. Do not manufacture candidates to fill a quota. If no question fits, return exactly [{"skip_reason": "<one short phrase>"}].
LOOKUP CONTRACT
Return two renderings of each question, word-identical except for one source-locator slot. In “question”, describe the TARGET by organ or author, type, relevant date/year and distinguishing subject. In “question_cited”, replace that entire slot with the exact official target identifier, retaining office/source-type words when attribution needs them. Resolution, report, letter and PV/SR meeting-record identifiers are allowed in the cited rendering. No document identifiers in the base rendering. Calendar dates and quantities are not identifiers.
Describe the actual source: a letter TO the Security Council is not authored BY it; an annexed draft is not an adopted declaration. A source locator is not necessarily the speaker. The substantive need, essential attribution and a sufficient anchor must remain understandable outside the slot in both renderings. A concrete subject such as UNAMIR, an identified refugee registration process or a particular sanctions measure may anchor the question; a generic “States” alone cannot. Both renderings must identify the same relevant time, using a year-bearing identifier or an absolute date outside the slot as needed.
Ask about one bounded fact, condition or connected procedural element. An interval or amount-with-unit is one fact. “What did the Security Council authorize for UNAMIR in [source]?” is valid; a vague “What does this document say?” is not. Requests and attributed proposals are eligible when asked with their actual status.
QUESTION TYPES
Use one of: sanction_condition_or_consequence, date_deadline_or_mandate, quantity_force_or_finance, reporting_monitoring_or_verification, actor_body_or_procedure, situation_scope_or_coverage, operative_action, finding_event_or_assessment. Choose the most descriptive category; categories have no priority or score advantage. When several questions are generated, prefer different facts and at least two categories when the block supports them; never pad for diversity.
EXAMPLE
Target: S/RES/918 (1994), adopted 17 May 1994: “Authorizes in this context an expansion of the UNAMIR force level up to 5,500 troops”.

This is a source-specific information need; the source name itself is not the answer.
