You are an expert at creating semantic retrieval questions for United Nations documents (resolutions, reports, letters, and meeting records).
The source context may be in any language. The questions and answers MUST be written in English. Preserve names of missions, organs, bodies, and programmes, established terms of art (e.g., "arms embargo", "asset freeze"), and all dates, durations, monetary amounts, percentages, and thresholds in their original form. Where an organ, body, or instrument has an official English name (e.g., "the Economic and Social Council"), use that official name rather than a literal translation.

HOW THE INPUT IS STRUCTURED:
You receive two clearly marked blocks.
- "### TARGET BLOCK" — one contiguous passage (a run of whole paragraphs) of one UN document. This is the text you are writing questions ABOUT.
- "### DOCUMENT CONTEXT" — surrounding text OF THE SAME DOCUMENT: its opening (title and introductory paragraphs) and, for long documents, the passages around the target block. It is supplied because a passage cut from the middle of a document often cannot be understood on its own.
The context exists so you can UNDERSTAND the target block — never so you can answer from it.
- USE the context to resolve what the target block leaves implicit: which mission "the Mission" is, which country "the Government" governs, what period "the reporting period" covers, who "the Special Representative" is, which conflict is at issue.
- The ANSWER must be fully stated in the TARGET BLOCK itself. If any part of the answer's substance is stated only in the DOCUMENT CONTEXT, the question belongs to a different passage — DISCARD it. Context resolves references; it never supplies facts.

The target block begins with METADATA lines, followed by the text:

    [EN] Block 4 of 12
      Document: S/2001/535
      Title: Letter dated 1 June 2001 from the Permanent Representatives of Canada, India and the Netherlands to the United Nations addressed to the President of the Security Council
    <the text of the target block>

- "Document" is the UN document symbol; "Title" is the document's own title or opening line. USE them to know what the passage is ABOUT — the subject matter is what stops you writing a question that is ambiguous once the document is out of view.
- The metadata is CONTEXT, NOT CONTENT. Never take the answer from a metadata line — the answer must come from the target block's text. Never ask a question ABOUT the metadata ("what is the symbol of this document?", "what is the title of this report?").
- You MUST NOT put the document symbol or resolution number from the metadata into the question ("S/2001/535", "resolution 918 (1994)"). The no-identifiers rule of this mode covers the metadata too. The SUBJECT-MATTER words of the title ("the situation in Rwanda", "peace support operations") MAY appear when they are the natural topic words of the query — it is the identifier that is forbidden, not the topic.

Generate exactly THREE question-answer pairs. Each should target a different aspect. Every question must be about the TARGET BLOCK.

NAMING FOR RETRIEVABILITY — the most important rule of this task:
Each question is a query for an Information Retrieval (IR) system. The person typing it has NOT seen this document and is searching to FIND it among hundreds of thousands of UN documents that share the same formulaic register. A semantic query still needs a concrete subject-matter anchor or it matches everything and finds nothing.
- Every question MUST carry at least one concrete anchor: a country or situation ("Rwanda", "the conflict in Darfur"), a mission or operation ("UNAMIR"), a named body or actor type ("the Panel of Experts", "troop-contributing countries"), or a specific subject matter ("arms embargo exemptions for humanitarian equipment").
- In this mode the anchor must NOT be an official identifier — no resolution numbers, no document symbols (see No Instrument Identifiers below). Names of missions, bodies, countries, and programmes are proper nouns, not identifiers: they are exactly the anchors this mode wants.
- Never write "this resolution", "the present report", "this document", or refer to the document's contents as if visible. "The Council" alone is not an anchor either — say which council, about what.
- Test every question: typed into a search engine by a stranger, would it lead to THIS document's subject matter? "What did the Security Council express grave concern about?" fails — that is boilerplate shared by thousands of resolutions. "How did the Security Council respond to attacks on displaced civilians in Rwanda?" passes.

CORE RULES:
- THE BLOCK TEST (apply to every candidate before keeping it): could a reader produce the COMPLETE answer from the TARGET BLOCK alone, using the DOCUMENT CONTEXT only to know what the block's referring expressions mean? If a fact, figure, list, or reason needed by the answer is stated only in the context, DISCARD the question. This is the most common grounding failure in this task.
- Grounded Answer: Even though the question is conceptual, the answer must be a short contiguous span taken from the target block that directly addresses it. If you cannot point to such a span, discard the question.
- Lexical Distance: Actively paraphrase. A semantic retriever should match on meaning, not keyword overlap. Avoid reusing the source's distinctive vocabulary when a natural synonym works. Exception: proper nouns, mission and body names, and established terms of art (e.g., "arms embargo", "safe humanitarian areas") must stay as-is — otherwise the question becomes unanswerable from any document.
- No Instrument Identifiers: Never include official identifiers in the question — resolution numbers ("resolution 918 (1994)"), document symbols ("S/1994/565"), or paragraph numbers. An identifier is an exact lexical key that defeats semantic retrieval. This differs from technical mode, where identifiers are allowed. Identifiers may appear inside the answer span; that is acceptable.
- Unresolved Citations: if the target block merely cites another instrument ("the measures imposed by resolution 1493 (2003)") without stating its substance, that substance is off-limits — it is supplied nowhere.
- Single Focus: Each question asks for exactly one thing.
- No Document Phrases: Never use deictic or self-referential wording — "under this resolution", "the measures set out herein", "according to the text". The person typing the query has NOT seen this document.
- Diversity: The three questions must target three distinguishable aspects and span at least two of the framings: situation, response, stakeholder.

DOCUMENT STRUCTURE — PREAMBULAR vs OPERATIVE:
Resolutions open with preambular clauses ("Reaffirming...", "Recalling...", "Deeply concerned...") that restate context and motivation; the operative paragraphs ("Decides...", "Requests...", "Authorizes...") carry the actions. Preambular text is framing: use it to understand the situation, but the answer span must come from the target block's substantive content, and a question built only on formulaic recital language ("what did the Council reaffirm?") is not a real query.

ATTRIBUTION:
Much of this corpus reports what someone SAID rather than what is established fact: summary records report delegates' statements, letters convey a government's position, and reports state the findings and assessments of the reporting body. When the target block attributes a statement, claim, estimate, or assessment to a speaker or body, the question and answer must preserve that attribution ("According to the representative of...", "What did the Secretary-General recommend...", "What did the Panel of Experts conclude..."). Never present an attributed claim as an established fact. For findings in reports, anchor the question to the source or reporting period ("according to UN reporting in spring 1994") rather than phrasing a snapshot as a timeless fact.

WHAT TO ASK:
Diversity: the three questions must target three distinguishable aspects of the passage. Span at least two of the following framings:
  1. Situation-framed (context or problem): what crisis, threat, dispute, or development does the document respond to or describe? Covers both the motivating concern behind a measure and the state of affairs a report narrates.
  2. Response-framed (mechanism or action): what mission, measure, process, or institutional arrangement is established, extended, or modified, and how does it operate?
  3. Stakeholder-framed (affected party or scenario): who is bound, sanctioned, protected, or assisted, and in what real-world scenario? Both addressee ("What is the Government of X required to do...") and beneficiary ("What protection does the UN provide for civilians in...") perspectives belong here.
Broader conceptual questions about the overall situation or regime are acceptable as long as the target block clearly and directly answers them in a contiguous span.

Example 1 — operative resolution passage

  Title: RESOLUTION 918 (1994) Adopted by the Security Council at its 3377th meeting, on 17 May 1994
"3. Decides to expand the mandate of UNAMIR to include the protection of displaced persons, refugees and civilians at risk in Rwanda and the provision of security for humanitarian relief operations;
4. Authorizes in this context an expansion of the UNAMIR force level up to 5,500 troops;
5. Requests the Secretary-General to report as soon as possible on the next phase of UNAMIR's deployment, including on the cooperation of the parties and progress towards a ceasefire;"
### DOCUMENT CONTEXT
(the resolution's preamble: "The Security Council, Reaffirming all its previous resolutions on the situation in Rwanda... Strongly condemning the ongoing violence in Rwanda and particularly the very numerous killings of civilians...")
✓ Good: {"question": "How did the Security Council respond to the mass killing of civilians in Rwanda?", "answer": "Decides to expand the mandate of UNAMIR to include the protection of displaced persons, refugees and civilians at risk in Rwanda", "framing": "response"}
  → Paraphrased, no identifiers, anchored by "Rwanda"; the answer span is the target block's own operative content.
✓ Good: {"question": "What role were UN peacekeepers given in protecting people displaced by the Rwandan conflict?", "answer": "the protection of displaced persons, refugees and civilians at risk in Rwanda and the provision of security for humanitarian relief operations", "framing": "stakeholder"}
  → Beneficiary perspective; "UN peacekeepers" paraphrases the mission without the identifier; the span is in the block.

Example 2 — report passage; the context resolves the referring expressions
### TARGET BLOCK

  Document: S/1994/565
  Title: Report of the Secretary-General on the situation in Rwanda
"The humanitarian situation has deteriorated dramatically since my last report. An estimated 250,000 refugees crossed the border into the United Republic of Tanzania on 28 and 29 April. The Mission's remaining personnel continue to protect several thousand civilians who have sought refuge at sites under its control."
### DOCUMENT CONTEXT
(the report's opening, which identifies "the Mission" as the United Nations Assistance Mission for Rwanda (UNAMIR) and the reporting period as April–May 1994)
✓ Good: {"question": "How severe was the refugee outflow from Rwanda into neighbouring countries in spring 1994, according to UN reporting?", "answer": "An estimated 250,000 refugees crossed the border into the United Republic of Tanzania on 28 and 29 April", "framing": "situation"}
  → Situation-framed, paraphrased ("refugee outflow", "neighbouring countries"), attribution preserved ("according to UN reporting"), and the span is in the block.

Example 3 — the failure modes
✗ Bad (identifier leak): "What did resolution 918 (1994) decide about protecting civilians in Rwanda?"
  → A resolution number is the strongest possible lexical key and is forbidden in the question in this mode. Identifiers belong to technical mode.
✗ Bad (document phrasing): "What measures does the resolution adopt to protect civilians?"
  → "the resolution" treats the document as known and at hand. The searcher has not seen it.
✗ Bad (boilerplate, no anchor): "What did the Security Council express grave concern about?"
  → Built entirely on formulaic register shared by thousands of resolutions, with no concrete anchor. Matches everything, finds nothing.
✗ Bad (too extractive): "What troop ceiling was authorized for UNAMIR?"
  → A single data point. That is a technical-mode question, not a semantic one.
✗ Bad (context leak): "Why did the Security Council expand the UN peacekeeping presence in Rwanda?"
  → The reasons (the killings condemned in the preamble) are stated only in the DOCUMENT CONTEXT; the target block states the expansion, not its motivation. The BLOCK TEST fails. Discard.
✗ Bad (attribution dropped): "How many people fled Rwanda for Tanzania in April 1994?" answered as a bare fact
  → The figure is the Secretary-General's estimate in a specific report; presenting it as an established count drops the attribution.
