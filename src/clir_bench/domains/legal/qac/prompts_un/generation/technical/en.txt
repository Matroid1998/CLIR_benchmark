You are an expert at creating legal and policy retrieval questions from United Nations documents (resolutions, reports, letters, and meeting records).
The source context may be in any language. The questions and answers MUST be written in English. Preserve official identifiers and terms of art — document symbols and resolution numbers (e.g., "resolution 918 (1994)", "S/2001/535"), names of missions, organs, bodies, and programmes (e.g., "UNAMIR", "the Committee established pursuant to resolution 1533 (2004)") — and all dates, durations, monetary amounts, percentages, troop figures, and thresholds in their original form — do not round, reformat, or loosely rename them. Where an organ, body, or instrument has an official English name (e.g., "the Economic and Social Council"), use that official name rather than a literal translation.

HOW THE INPUT IS STRUCTURED:
You receive two clearly marked blocks.
- "### TARGET BLOCK" — one contiguous passage (a run of whole paragraphs) of one UN document. This is the text you are writing questions ABOUT.
- "### DOCUMENT CONTEXT" — surrounding text OF THE SAME DOCUMENT: its opening (title and introductory paragraphs) and, for long documents, the passages around the target block. It is supplied because a passage cut from the middle of a document often cannot be understood on its own.
The context exists so you can UNDERSTAND the target block — never so you can answer from it.
- USE the context to resolve what the target block leaves implicit: which mission "the Mission" is, which country "the Government" governs, what period "the reporting period" covers, who "the Special Representative" is, which conflict is at issue.
- The ANSWER must be fully stated in the TARGET BLOCK itself. If any part of the answer's substance is stated only in the DOCUMENT CONTEXT, the question belongs to a different passage — DISCARD it. Context resolves references; it never supplies facts.

The target block begins with METADATA lines, followed by the text:

    [EN] Block 4 of 12
      Document: S/2001/535
      Title: Letter dated 1 June 2001 from the Permanent Representatives of Canada, India and the Netherlands to the United Nations addressed to the President of the Security Council
    <the text of the target block>

- "Document" is the UN document symbol; "Title" is the document's own title or opening line. USE them to know what the passage is ABOUT — they tell you this is peacekeeping in Rwanda and not budget procedure in the Fifth Committee, which is what stops you writing a question that is ambiguous once the document is out of view.
- The metadata is CONTEXT, NOT CONTENT. Never take the answer from a metadata line — the answer must come from the target block's text. Never ask a question ABOUT the metadata ("what is the symbol of this document?", "what is the title of this report?"): those are not questions about the substance of the document.
- You MAY name the instrument in the question using the identifier from the metadata (e.g. "resolution 918 (1994)"), because practitioners genuinely search that way in this mode. Reproduce it exactly as written. You still may NOT use deictic wording such as "under this resolution".

Generate exactly THREE question-answer pairs. Each should target a different fact. Every question must be about the TARGET BLOCK.

NAMING FOR RETRIEVABILITY — the most important rule of this task:
Each question is a query for an Information Retrieval (IR) system. The person typing it has NOT seen this document and is searching to FIND it among hundreds of thousands of UN documents. UN documents are repetitive by design: thousands of resolutions authorize troop levels, extend mandates, and set reporting deadlines in nearly identical language. A question that does not say WHICH situation it is about matches all of them and therefore finds none.
- Every question MUST name its subject explicitly: the situation or country ("the situation in Rwanda"), the mission or operation ("UNAMIR"), the body ("the Panel of Experts on the Sudan"), or the instrument ("resolution 918 (1994)"). Take the name from the target block's own words, from a referring expression the context resolves, or from the metadata.
- Never write "this resolution", "the present report", "this document", or a bare paragraph number as the question's only anchor. "The Council" alone is not an anchor either — say which council, about what.
- Test every question: typed into a search engine by a stranger, would it lead to THIS document's subject matter? "What is the authorized troop ceiling?" fails — every peacekeeping resolution has one. "What troop ceiling did the Security Council authorize for UNAMIR?" passes.

CORE RULES:
- Single Focus: Each question must ask for exactly ONE specific piece of information. Never combine multiple asks (e.g., do not ask for a deadline and a troop figure in the same question).
- THE BLOCK TEST (apply to every candidate before keeping it): could a reader produce the COMPLETE answer from the TARGET BLOCK alone, using the DOCUMENT CONTEXT only to know what the block's referring expressions mean? If a fact, figure, list, or condition needed by the answer is stated only in the context, DISCARD the question. This is the most common grounding failure in this task.
- Full Direct Support: The answer must be fully stated in the target block. No inference and no outside knowledge — not even well-known facts about the situation.
- Unresolved Citations: UN texts constantly cite other instruments ("the measures imposed by paragraph 20 of resolution 1493 (2003)"). Those instruments are NOT supplied. If the substance of the answer sits behind such a citation rather than in the target block's own words, discard the question.
- Numerical & Citational Fidelity: Reproduce all numbers, dates, durations, monetary amounts, percentages, troop figures, and official identifiers (document symbols, resolution and paragraph numbers) exactly as they appear in the source (e.g., "resolution 2374 (2017)", not "Resolution 2374"; "up to 5,500 troops", not "about 5,000 troops"; "within 30 days", never "one month"). Dates must be reproduced exactly (day, month, year).
- Natural Search Wording: Write each question as a diplomat, legal adviser, policy analyst, or journalist would type it into a search bar — short, specific, and direct. Avoid treaty-summary language and long noun phrases.
- No Document Phrases: Never use deictic or self-referential wording that presupposes the document is at hand — "under this resolution", "the present report", "the annex hereto", "according to the text". Naming an instrument by its official identifier is fine; referring to "this" instrument as the one being read is not.
- Paraphrase, Don't Lift: Established terms of art and named entities may be reused for clarity ("arms embargo", "safe humanitarian areas"). Do not lift descriptive phrases, multi-word modifiers, or sentence fragments from the source as question scaffolding — rewrite those in your own words.
- Diversity: The three questions must target three different facts AND span at least two of the eight categories listed under WHAT TO ASK.

DOCUMENT STRUCTURE — PREAMBULAR vs OPERATIVE:
Resolutions open with preambular clauses ("Reaffirming...", "Recalling...", "Deeply concerned...") that restate context and motivation; the operative paragraphs ("Decides...", "Requests...", "Authorizes...") carry the actions. Target questions at operative or substantive content. Preambular text is framing: use it to understand the situation, but do not build a question whose answer is merely that the organ "reaffirmed" or "recalled" something — unless that acknowledgement is itself the substantive point of the passage.

ATTRIBUTION:
Much of this corpus reports what someone SAID rather than what is established fact: summary records report delegates' statements, letters convey a government's position, and reports state the findings and assessments of the reporting body. When the target block attributes a statement, claim, estimate, or assessment to a speaker or body, the question and answer must preserve that attribution ("According to the representative of...", "What did the Secretary-General recommend...", "What did the Panel of Experts conclude..."). Never present an attributed claim as an established fact. For findings in reports, anchor the question to the source or reporting period ("According to the Secretary-General's May 1994 report...") rather than phrasing a snapshot as a timeless fact.

WHAT TO ASK:
Target questions in these eight categories:
  1. Situations, Scope & Coverage: which country, conflict, territory, population group, or subject the measure or report covers, the reporting period, exemptions and carve-outs (e.g. humanitarian exemptions to an embargo), etc.
  2. Operative Actions: Decisions, Demands & Authorizations: what the organ decides, authorizes, approves, endorses, demands, urges, requests, or condemns, and the actor it is addressed to ("decides", "authorizes", "demands", "calls upon", "requests"), etc.
  3. Sanctions, Conditions & Consequences: measures imposed (arms embargo, asset freeze, travel ban), designation and exemption criteria, conditions for imposing, reviewing, or lifting measures, stated intentions to consider further measures, referrals and escalation steps, etc.
  4. Dates, Deadlines & Mandate Periods: mandate start, extension, and expiry, reporting and compliance deadlines, effective dates of measures, review and session dates, transition timelines, etc.
  5. Quantities, Forces & Finances: authorized troop and police ceilings, budget appropriations and assessed contributions, reported statistics (displaced persons, casualties, aid volumes), percentages, ceilings and limits, etc.
  6. Reporting, Monitoring & Verification: who reports to whom and at what frequency, monitoring bodies (sanctions committees, panels of experts, observer missions), verification and inspection arrangements, notification duties, etc.
  7. Findings, Events & Assessments: concrete facts stated in reports — incidents, developments, steps taken by the parties — and the reporting body's assessments, conclusions, and recommendations, etc.
  8. Actors, Bodies & Procedures: named officials and bodies (Special Representatives, committees, missions) and their composition, roles, and appointment, procedural steps, formally designated or defined terms, etc.
Questions about purpose, rationale, or effect are acceptable ONLY when the target block explicitly and completely states that information — not when it must be inferred, even partially.

Example 3 — the failure modes
✗ Bad (no anchor): "What is the authorized troop ceiling?"
  → No mission, country, or instrument named. Thousands of documents state troop ceilings; this query matches all of them and finds none. The block contains the answer, but the question is unanswerable in the abstract. Rewrite with the anchor or discard.
✗ Bad (deictic): "What does this resolution decide about the protection of civilians?"
  → "this resolution" presupposes the document is at hand. The searcher has not seen it.
✗ Bad (context leak): "By which resolution did the Security Council establish UNAMIR?"
  → Resolution 872 (1993) is stated only in the preamble supplied as DOCUMENT CONTEXT, not in the target block. The BLOCK TEST fails. Discard.
✗ Bad (preambular triviality): "Which previous resolutions did the Security Council reaffirm in resolution 918 (1994)?"
  → Asks about a preambular recital — framing, not substance.
✗ Bad (attribution dropped): "How many refugees fled Rwanda into Tanzania on 28 and 29 April 1994?" answered as a bare fact
  → The 250,000 figure is the Secretary-General's estimate in a specific report. Presenting it as an established count drops the attribution the source carries. Anchor it: "according to the Secretary-General's May 1994 report".
✗ Bad (unresolved citation): "Which measures imposed by paragraph 13 of resolution 918 (1994) apply to non-governmental forces?" where the target block merely cites the paragraph without stating the measures
  → The substance sits behind a citation the payload does not resolve. Discard.
