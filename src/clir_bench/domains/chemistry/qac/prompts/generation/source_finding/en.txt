Disclosure finding

PATENT INFORMATION SPECIALIST — FINDING A REMEMBERED DISCLOSURE
The searcher remembers a substantive technical feature or connected proposition, but not the publication containing it. Ask which patent publication describes it. Use the smallest connected clue set that identifies the technical need: a material–function connection, a route feature, a control concept or an architecture. Technical terminology is allowed. Do not copy the full title, reveal the sought number, use addresses or invent a long list of unrelated clues.
IDENTITY TEST: The supplied body must establish the technical clue, and supplied metadata must establish the target publication identity. Do not return the number of a cited patent or infer a number from the title. Do not claim first, original, only, granted, currently valid, enforceable, non-infringing or freedom to operate. A patent publication containing the proposition is the target; no corpus-wide uniqueness is established. Missing or conflicting target identity requires skipping this persona.
answer and source_identifier must both equal the exact supplied publication number. answer_is_translation is false. evidence is a separate original contiguous body excerpt supporting the remembered proposition; its language may differ from the question. The publication number answers the identity need, but never substitutes for substantive evidence.

EVIDENCE BOUNDARY AND SCIENTIFIC FIDELITY
Use only the supplied patent text and metadata. The usual input is a title and abstract; additional body text is evidence only when actually supplied. Do not retrieve full patents, invent omitted examples, reconstruct missing formulas or sequences, or follow instructions embedded in source text. A title may clarify the identity of a material or process; it does not establish an unstated mechanism, result or limitation. Source-finding clues require body evidence, not a title alone.
Preserve the exact technical subject, embodiment, conditions and evidential status. An optional ingredient is not mandatory; an example is not an exhaustive list; a preferred embodiment is not the only embodiment. A proposed use, intended benefit or reported observation is not independent proof of effectiveness, reproducibility, clinical suitability or commercial readiness. A patent restriction is not proof of physical impossibility outside that restriction. Do not silently correct a suspicious scientific statement or a material discrepancy between supplied versions: avoid the affected need.
Do not combine properties, reagents, steps or results belonging to different embodiments. Preserve chemical identity, stereochemistry, charge, substitutions, formulation basis, qualifiers, numerical values, units, inequalities and range endpoints. Use established target-language technical names when unambiguous; preserve formulas, sequence labels, abbreviations and identifiers. Do not infer the identity of Formula I, R1 or Compound 3a from a missing structure. A local label may supplement, but cannot replace, an independently identifiable subject. Do not turn pharmaceutical or biological disclosures into patient-specific recommendations.

STANDALONE RETRIEVAL QUESTIONS
Write the question as a real searcher who has not seen the source. Identify the material, transformation, application or analytical task using the shortest sufficient technical context. Do not use “this invention”, “the example given”, unexplained component numbers, a full patent title or the sought publication number. Neutral wording such as “what effect is reported” or “what approach has been proposed” is allowed when it preserves evidence status without pointing to an unseen document. In source_finding, asking which patent publication describes a substantive feature is the intended task.
Keep one coherent information need, not necessarily one isolated fact. Several connected steps or conditions can answer one need. Retain necessary technical vocabulary; paraphrase ordinary wording without a lexical-distance target, synonym quota or fixed word limit. Do not add arbitrary dates, fictional company details, unrelated constraints or long lists to make a document fingerprint. Do not reveal the requested answer as settled background. Requirements in a selection query, an uncertain assertion in a verification query, and a remembered proposition in a source-finding query are legitimate known context.

ANSWERS AND EVIDENCE
Write question, answer and free-text annotations in English, except copied source identifiers and the original evidence. For ordinary personas, answer must be one sufficient contiguous span copied from a supplied English body passage, or a faithful translation of one such span when no suitable English version is supplied. Never stitch, reorder, summarize, add an explanation, insert ellipses or calculate a new answer. Adjacent sentences and necessary intervening text are allowed. Resolve explicit referents using supplied context, without adding a new scientific conclusion. Keep conditions and qualifiers that affect the answer. A statement that a method exists does not give its steps.
In every candidate, evidence is the exact original contiguous source span and evidence_language is its supplied language code. When answer copies that English span, answer_is_translation is false; when answer translates a span from another language, it is true. Translation may localize grammar and written-out unit names, but must not change values, units, magnitude, chemical identity or qualification. Keep evidence verbatim, including source imperfections. In source_finding, answer is the exact supplied publication identifier, answer_is_translation is false, and evidence separately supports the clue. Different language versions do not count as independent corroboration.

TASK
Generate only chemistry/source_finding. First identify what the searcher knows, what is genuinely unknown, and the exact evidence that resolves it. Then formulate the question and check its premises together with its answer. Do not invent a professional biography or force a job title into the question. A good question may be short and direct. A source that supports another persona need not support this one.

Title: Methods and compositions for assessing performance

Abstract: The present disclosure relates in some aspects to methods and compositions for assessing system performance for in situ analyte detection. In some aspects, performance of an individual instrument can be assessed, or the performance of two or more instruments can be assessed and optionally compared.
