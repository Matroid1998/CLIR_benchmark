Blind UN-A review notes — reviewer of packet indices 0–6 only

Scope and method
I read the seven assigned target blocks and all 162 supplied question/answer pairs, across 21 document/mode families. I consulted source_payload only for attribution, episode identity and whether an answer was target substance or surrounding context. I did not open mapping_PRIVATE.json, prompt versions or automated score files. Group letters are randomized per document/mode and must not be aggregated as versions. Indices 7–9 were reassigned to another reviewer and are not covered by this file.

Ratings concern the question as an information need answerable from its assigned target. Answer defects are marked separately. I did not penalize yes/no form, extractive asks, speeches, preambular policy statements, a particular mode label, an arbitrary word count or a fixed number of anchors. A missing date lowered a rating only where the changing historical episode materially affected interpretation. Several UNIDO questions without a year receive 5 because the institution, regulation and issue identify the need adequately. Empty groups are abstentions, not zeros. Preferred sets reflect their general quality and consistency; a single strong candidate can win on quality while supplying less coverage.

Strongest matched comparisons
1. S/2004/505#15, semantic: C1/C2 clearly identify the 2004 non-proliferation regime and ask target-supported implementation and legal-effect questions. A1/A3 and B2/B3 leave the particular resolution or assessment implicit. B1 asks a legitimate business-policy question, but its substantive answer is in surrounding paragraphs 40–41; the assigned block lists guests and the meeting topic. This is a target mismatch, not invented business-policy content.
2. GC.7/C.1/SR.5#16: lookup A and C contain strong attributed questions about tax reimbursement and amendment procedure. Lookup B2's nationality clarification and semantic C1's explanation of acquired-rights prejudice come from surrounding context, not the target. Practitioner B and C are consistently strong; C1's yes/no suspension question is a useful legal check, not inherently inferior to an open question.
3. CEDAW/C/SR.434#16, lookup: A1 and A3 ask exactly what information the expert requested and are strong. A2 asks how the Government was asked to convey its intention, while the passage only records a question asking how it planned to convey it. That wording mistakes an unanswered request for a supplied method. Semantic C consistently preserves a useful historical policy/rights information need; the remedy must not be flattened into an enacted new CEDAW procedure.
4. CD/PV.880#1: lookup A2/A3 identify the Conference on Disarmament and its 880th meeting, whereas several practitioner/semantic questions mention only the President or the Conference. Semantic B1 changes the Conference's final working weeks to the final weeks of the calendar year 2001. These are meaningful contextual weaknesses rather than penalties for short phrasing or absent formal citation.
5. A/C.5/60/SR.64#14: most sets supply strong, attributed questions about Pakistan's assessment and Argentina's promised consultations. Semantic A1 over-asks what strengthening was considered necessary: the target only says UNAMA needed strengthening; proposed concrete measures occur later. Both short yes/no questions and richer policy questions can be good here.
6. A/RES/60/47#0: every nonempty lookup set is strong, and semantic A/C are tied in quality. The preamble contains real reporting, significance and management-principle information. Treating all such material as automatically unsuitable would discard usable questions. Practitioner B3 is weaker because it never says the recalled report concerns Antarctica.
7. A/RES/68/143#7: all lookup sets are strong, including the one-candidate set. Some practitioner questions obscure the subject behind the resolution or the 2013 framework; others are fully usable practical duty questions. Neither more candidates nor semantic wording alone indicates higher question quality.

Blind overall impression
There is substantial overlap in quality between versions' candidate sets, with many near-equivalent strong questions. The decisive failures are specific: unresolved institution/instrument/episode references, demand for an answer not in the selected block, occasional shifts from a conditional or reported statement into an established event, and questions so closely framed around an unanswered request that no substantive answer follows. Concise factual and yes/no asks often work well. Richer questions work well when their added detail is actually supported; they also create more opportunities to over-ask or import context. I cannot name a winning version while blinded. Parent should map labels only after combining reviews and keep coverage, question quality and supplied-answer defects separate.

Limitations
One reviewer per assigned pair, subjective 1–5 scale, and a small selected document sample rather than a random corpus-wide assessment. Some historical metadata are incomplete: the UNIDO header does not independently establish an exact meeting date, and I did not manufacture a date error from its archive path. A context-only answer can be factually true while invalid for the assigned target. Set preference is not a claim that a mode or backend is intrinsically better. Reasons and full per-candidate ratings are in un_a_reviews.json.
