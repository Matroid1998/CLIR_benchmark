Blind manual question review: UN packet B, document indices 0–6

Scope: 7 documents, 21 document/mode families, all 63 blinded groups, 161 questions. Every supplied question has an individual 1–5 rating and a separate answer status. Empty groups are abstentions, not zero. The parent reviewer is independently handling document indices 7–9; those are not included in un_b_reviews.json at this handoff.

Method: Read each target block before its candidates and used surrounding source text only to resolve identity, date, attribution and the scope of the excerpt. Did not open mapping_PRIVATE.json, automated scores or prompt versions. Labels A/B/C are independently randomized by document/mode and cannot be aggregated as if they were versions. Set preferences consider consistency, naturalness, substantive value and important defects across the whole set, rather than only the maximum rating or number of candidates. More candidates are not automatically better; ties are retained when warranted.

Strong matched comparisons:
- S/2012/718 lookup: B consistently asks unknown substantive facts (tracing locations, accountability conduct and follow-up), whereas A3 puts essentially the complete accountability concern into the question. Practitioner B3 omits Sri Lanka and leaves both the Government and conflict unidentified. This is a retrieval-context defect, not a missing-date quota.
- CEDAW/C/SR.724 lookup B2: paragraph 48 continues Ms Tan's remarks from paragraph 47; Ms Zou first speaks in paragraph 49. The question's speaker attribution is wrong, although the quoted request itself is real. A2 preserves that the maternal-mortality figure was something Ms Zou had heard, rather than asserting that it was a verified rate.
- Colombia CEDAW review: questions asking what leaders would be required to do preserve the source. Practitioner A3 and semantic A1 instead ask what Colombia already required; the source only describes a prospective requirement. Semantic C asks two coherent substantive policy questions about support and rationale without that change in status.
- Human Rights Committee drafting: lookup A/B and practitioner A ask meaningful questions about proposals, drafting reasons and placement. Semantic B1 gives only undefined Committee/paragraph references and receives an answer with unresolved 'that approach'. A quoted speech or procedural question is not inherently low-value; identifying the actual issue is what matters.
- CD/PV.841: lookup B has consistently strong attributed policy/procedure questions. Lookup A1 and B3 both deserve question rating 5, despite major answer defects: 'such an assurance' omits the assurance being asked for. Semantic A2 is also a strong rationale question with an answer that omits the adjacent explicit FMCT link. Questions about undefined 'subsequent consultations' or an anonymous supposedly necessary global condition need context. These examples show why question quality and evidence-span quality must be separated.
- Register of Conventional Arms: all three lookup sets are strong; there is no forced winner. Practitioner B1 shifts report preparation from the Secretary-General to the assisting experts. Practitioner C3 lists all the requested view topics in the question, making its answer largely circular. Specific factual questions are useful here without needing an invented scenario.
- Peacekeeping support account: practitioner C is consistently self-contained; B also has two excellent questions, especially its explicitly partial-financing question. Lookup A2 asks for the financing scheme but its answer supplies only subparagraph 15(a); later context contains other components. This is incomplete target coverage, not an invented amount. Questions about approval, taking note of a report and staffing are legitimate substantive needs; their quality depends on wording and context, not the operative verb alone.

Blind impressions: No question style wins automatically. Concise facts can be excellent; connected rationale/process questions can also be excellent when the target actually explains them. The clearest weaknesses are circular requests, missing episode identity, unsupported shifts from reported/proposed to implemented, wrong speakers, and requests for explanation where the excerpt merely announces a result. A well-written question may still be paired with a poor answer span. Some set preferences are modest or tied, and should not be presented as decisive version effects before labels are unblinded by the parent.

Limits: This is one agent's qualitative review, not an independent human expert validation or measured retrieval experiment. The documents are a diagnostic subset, not a random corpus prevalence sample. Only English questions are reviewed. Context and usefulness judgments involve discretion; a 4 versus 5 distinction is less robust than a clear wrong-speaker or unresolved-reference defect. Prior familiarity with some source episodes is possible, but hidden version mapping and automated ratings were not consulted for this review. Abstention coverage and question quality should be reported separately.
